💳 Money Tips
Always pay in YEN — never USD. When a terminal asks "Pay in USD or JPY?" — always choose JPY. Every single time. Paying in USD adds a hidden 3–6% markup called dynamic currency conversion.
  • Visa and Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere that takes cards
  • American Express works at hotels, department stores, and larger restaurants only
  • Many small restaurants, shrines, temples, and rural shops are cash only
  • 7-Eleven (7-Bank) and Lawson ATMs reliably accept US cards — English menus available
  • Coins are high-value — ¥500 ≈ $3.25, ¥100 ≈ $0.65. Use them constantly.
  • Coins cannot be exchanged back to USD at banks — spend them before flying home
  • Show your passport at larger stores for 8–10% tax refund on purchases over ¥5,000
  • Banks in Japan close at 3:00pm and are closed on weekends

🚕 Taxis
  • Doors open and close automatically. Never touch the door — the driver operates it. Opening it yourself is a serious faux pas.
  • No tipping — ever. Leaving money is considered rude. Pay the meter and go.
  • Hailing one: Raise your hand at the curb. A red light on the dashboard means available. Green means occupied. Taxi stands are at all major stations and airports.
  • English is rare. Always have your destination written in Japanese or pulled up on Google Maps to show the driver. Screenshot it before you get in.
  • Payment: Cash preferred, but IC cards are increasingly accepted. Credit cards hit or miss — confirm before you get in. Always have yen as backup.
  • Base fare: Around ¥500–730 to start, then ¥80–100 per 300–400m. Tokyo is expensive. A short 10-min ride can be ¥1,500+.
At major airports (Narita, Haneda, Kansai, Fukuoka) there are fixed-fare taxis to central areas — look for the fixed-fare desk. More expensive than rail but easier with heavy bags.
📱 Rideshare & App-Based Services
Japan rideshare ≠ Western rideshare. Uber and DiDi dispatch licensed taxis — not private drivers. Expect a taxi to show up, driven by a professional driver in white gloves.
  • GO App — the dominant rideshare app in Japan. Works in most major cities. Download before you arrive. Japanese taxis dispatched, English interface. Best for Fukuoka, Osaka, Tokyo.
  • Uber — limited coverage. Available in Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and a few other cities. More expensive than GO. Useful as backup.
  • DiDi — available in major cities. Similar to GO. Less popular but reliable.
  • S.RIDE — Tokyo-focused. Good for Haneda/Narita airport pickups.
App-based services shine late at night when street taxis are scarce. In smaller cities like Beppu, taxis at stands are more reliable than apps.
🚆 Rail System
Most Important

IC Cards — Suica, ICOCA, Pasmo

  • Get one immediately on arrival. IC cards work on almost all trains, subways, and buses in Japan and at many convenience stores and vending machines.
  • Suica (JR East, Tokyo) and ICOCA (JR West, Osaka/Kyoto) are the most versatile. Suica works nationally. Either card works almost everywhere.
  • Add Suica to Apple Wallet or Google Pay before you leave the US — tap your phone at any gate. No physical card needed. Works on iPhone 8+ and most Android flagships.
  • Load cash onto the card at any ticket machine or convenience store. Start with ¥3,000–5,000. Top up at machines inside stations.
  • At the gate: tap in, tap out. The system calculates the exact fare automatically. No need to buy a ticket for short trips.
  • Balance check: any ticket machine shows your balance. Convenience store cashiers can also check it.

Shinkansen (Bullet Train)

  • Book reserved seats in advance for long trips. Unreserved cars exist but can be standing-room-only on busy days and holidays.
  • Oversized luggage rule: Bags larger than 160cm total (L+W+H) must be reserved to a designated space. Book at the ticket counter when buying your ticket.
  • Buy tickets: at any JR ticket office (green windows), ticket machines, or online at smart-ex.jp. Smart-EX lets you book and change tickets on your phone.
  • Boarding: yellow line markers on the platform show exactly where each car door will stop. Line up behind them. Trains are precise to the second.
  • Nozomi and Mizuho are the fastest trains but NOT covered by the standard JR Pass — you pay a surcharge or buy a separate ticket.
  • Hikari and Sakura are slightly slower but JR Pass-valid. The time difference is usually under 30 minutes.

Regional & Local Lines

  • JR vs private lines: JR (Japan Rail) covers most long-distance routes. Private lines (Hankyu, Kintetsu, Keio, Odakyu) often serve city centers more directly — and are cheaper. Your IC card works on both.
  • Tokyo — Yamanote Line is the green loop that connects all major hubs: Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Ueno, Akihabara, Tokyo Station. Learn this line first.
  • Osaka — Loop Line circles the city similarly. Osaka Metro (subway) fills in the gaps. IC card works on all.
  • Kyoto — buses cover most tourist areas. The subway is limited. Buy a day bus pass (¥700) at the station or tourist office if doing multiple bus trips.
  • Hiroshima — trams (streetcars) are the main way to get around the city and to the ferry for Miyajima. Easy flat fare of ¥190. IC cards accepted.
  • Signs: All major stations have English signage and platform signs. Follow the color-coded line maps. If confused, look up on Google Maps before you move.

Recommended Apps

  • Google Maps — reliable for most transit routing. Shows platform numbers, transfer instructions, and IC card fares. Best for casual use.
  • Hyperdia (hyperdia.com) — more detailed than Google Maps for complex rail journeys. Shows every train option, exact fares, and JR Pass validity. Use for Shinkansen planning.
  • Japan Official Travel App — offline maps, route search, and tourist info. Good backup when you don't have data.
  • Navitime Japan — tells you which car to board for the best exit at your destination. Saves time at busy stations.
✈ Domestic Flights

Flying makes sense when the Shinkansen journey is over 3 hours — typically routes to/from Hokkaido, Okinawa, southern Kyushu, or when fares are dramatically cheaper.

  • LCCs (budget airlines): Peach Aviation, Jetstar Japan, Skymark. Fares can be ¥3,000–8,000 on sale. Watch baggage fees — they add up fast. Often cheaper booked direct on airline site.
  • Full-service: ANA and JAL. More reliable, better luggage allowance, sometimes comparable prices if booked early. ANA Experience Japan Fare offers discounted domestic tickets for foreign visitors — book before leaving the US.
  • Main domestic hubs: Haneda (HND) for Tokyo domestic — closer to the city than Narita. Itami (ITM) for Osaka/Kyoto. Fukuoka (FUK). Naha (OKA) for Okinawa. New Chitose (CTS) for Sapporo.
  • Punctuality: Japanese domestic flights are extremely on time. Budget 90 minutes before departure — security is fast, airports are efficient.
  • Owen's note: For the July 25 Tokyo → Fukuoka leg, ANA or Peach are both solid. Book ANA via the Experience Japan Fare for the best value.
💴 Costs & Payment
  • Japan is still heavily cash-based. Always carry yen. Many small restaurants, local buses, taxis, shrines, and rural shops are cash only.
  • Best ATMs for foreign cards: 7-Eleven (7-Bank) and Japan Post Bank. Available 24/7. English interface. Accept Visa, Mastercard, most US debit cards.
  • No tipping. Ever. Not for taxis, not for restaurants, not for porters, not for anyone. It is not customary and can cause confusion or embarrassment.
  • IC card is the easiest way to pay for all transit — tap in, tap out, exact fare charged automatically. Load it and forget about buying tickets for most trips.

📦 Luggage Forwarding (Takuhaibin)

One of Japan's best-kept travel secrets. Send your bags ahead to your next hotel — they arrive the next day, for around ¥1,500–2,500 per bag.

  • Yamato Transport (ヤマト) is the main service. Drop off at any convenience store (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson) or hotel front desk.
  • How to use: Fill out the slip at the convenience store. They'll scan it and give you a tracking number. Bag arrives at your next hotel next morning.
  • Why it matters: Shinkansen overhead racks are small. Traveling with a backpack while your main bag travels ahead makes train stations, trams, and subways dramatically easier.
  • Plan ahead: Ship the bag 2 days before you need it if doing same-day travel. Most hotels will hold bags arriving before check-in.
🗣 Language & Getting Around
  • Most drivers and station staff speak little to no English. This is fine — Japan is navigable without it.
  • Google Translate camera mode translates signs and menus in real time. Download the Japanese offline pack before you leave.
  • Always have your destination in Japanese on your phone — screenshot from Google Maps or save the Japanese address. Show it to the driver or station staff.
  • Use visual apps like Google Maps or Navitime that show routes graphically — you can follow the route without reading any Japanese.
  • Station staff are helpful. If you look lost, someone will often approach to help. Pointing at your phone map is universal.
📋 Transit Etiquette
  • No phone calls on trains. Put phone on silent (manner mode). Texting is fine. Speaking loudly is not.
  • Don't eat or drink while walking — it's considered rude in most public spaces. Eat at your seat on Shinkansen.
  • Escalators: Stand on the left in Tokyo, stand on the right in Osaka. The other side is for walking. Get this wrong and you'll block commuters.
  • Priority seats (near train doors, usually marked in a different color) — give these up for elderly, pregnant, or disabled passengers without being asked.
  • Backpacks: take your backpack off and hold it in front of you in crowded trains. It's considered courteous.
  • Queue at the platform markers. Lines form orderly at the door markers. Don't push. Trains are reliably on time — you will make it.

📋 Cultural Essentials
  • No tipping — ever. Restaurants, taxis, hotels.
  • Shoes off in dorm rooms, tatami areas, many restaurants.
  • Trains are silent. No calls. Manner mode (マナーモード).
  • Cars drive on the LEFT. Look right before stepping off curbs.
  • Coins are high-value. ¥500 = ~$3.25. Use them constantly.
  • Tax-free shopping. Show passport for 8–10% refund over ¥5,000.
  • Onsen: Shower first. No swimwear. No towels in the water.
  • Trash cans are rare — carry a small bag for wrappers.

💳 Money Tips
Always pay in YEN — never USD. When a terminal asks "Pay in USD or JPY?" — always choose JPY. Every single time. Paying in USD adds a hidden 3–6% markup called dynamic currency conversion.
  • Visa and Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere that takes cards
  • American Express works at hotels, department stores, and larger restaurants only
  • Many small restaurants, shrines, temples, and rural shops are cash only
  • 7-Eleven (7-Bank) and Lawson ATMs reliably accept US cards — English menus available
  • Coins are high-value — ¥500 ≈ $3.25, ¥100 ≈ $0.65. Use them constantly.
  • Coins cannot be exchanged back to USD at banks — spend them before flying home
  • Show your passport at larger stores for 8–10% tax refund on purchases over ¥5,000
  • Banks in Japan close at 3:00pm and are closed on weekends

📋 Cultural Essentials
  • No tipping — ever. Restaurants, taxis, hotels.
  • Shoes off in dorm rooms, tatami areas, many restaurants.
  • Trains are silent. No calls. Manner mode (マナーモード).
  • Cars drive on the LEFT. Look right before stepping off curbs.
  • Coins are high-value. ¥500 = ~$3.25. Use them constantly.
  • Tax-free shopping. Show passport for 8–10% refund over ¥5,000.
  • Onsen: Shower first. No swimwear. No towels in the water.
  • Trash cans are rare — carry a small bag for wrappers.
